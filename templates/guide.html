1. Options Basics

What is an Option?

An option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a specified price (the strike price) before a certain date (the expiry).

Call Option (CE) — Right to buy at the strike price.
If you sell a call, you receive premium upfront but are obligated to sell the stock at the strike if it goes above.

Put Option (PE) — Right to sell at the strike price.
If you buy a put, you pay premium but get downside protection — you can sell at the strike even if the stock crashes.

Key Terms

Strike Price (K)The price at which the option can be exercised
PremiumThe price paid/received for the option contract
ExpiryThe date the option expires (monthly in our strategy)
ATM (At The Money)Strike price ≈ current stock price
OTM (Out of The Money)Call: strike > stock price. Put: strike < stock price
ITM (In The Money)Call: strike < stock price. Put: strike > stock price
Lot SizeMinimum quantity per options contract (e.g., NIFTY = 75 units)

Moneyness Example

NIFTY at 22,000 Call Strike 22,500 → OTM by 2.3% (stock needs to rise for this to have value) Call Strike 22,000 → ATM (at the money) Call Strike 21,500 → ITM by 2.3% (already has intrinsic value) Put Strike 21,500 → OTM by 2.3% (stock needs to fall for this to have value) Put Strike 22,000 → ATM Put Strike 22,500 → ITM by 2.3%

2. The Greeks & Implied Volatility

Implied Volatility (IV)

Implied Volatility is the market's expectation of how much a stock will move. It's embedded in option prices — higher IV means higher premiums.

Historical Vol vs Implied Vol
Historical volatility measures how much the stock actually moved in the past.
Implied volatility measures how much the market expects it to move in the future.

IV is almost always higher than historical vol. This gap is the Volatility Risk Premium — and it's why selling options is profitable.

India VIX

The India VIX is the "fear index" — it measures the overall implied volatility of NIFTY options. When VIX is high (>18), option premiums are rich and selling is more attractive. When VIX is low (<12), premiums are thin.

IV Skew (Smile)

In theory, all options at the same expiry should have the same IV. In practice, OTM puts have higher IV than ATM options (people pay extra for crash protection). This creates a "skew" or "smile" pattern. Our model accounts for this.

Key Greeks

Delta (Δ)How much the option price changes per ₹1 move in stock. A call with delta 0.3 moves ₹0.30 per ₹1 stock move.
Theta (Θ)How much the option loses per day from time decay. As an option seller, theta works in your favor.
Vega (v)How much the option price changes per 1% change in IV. High vega = more sensitivity to vol changes.
Gamma (Γ)Rate of change of delta. High gamma near expiry means sudden large moves can hurt.

3. The Collar Strategy

What is a Collar?

A collar is a 3-legged position that generates steady income while limiting both upside and downside:

Collar = Buy Stock + Sell OTM Call + Buy OTM Put

1. Buy the stock/index at current price
2. Sell a call above the current price → receive premium (your income)
3. Buy a put below the current price → pay premium (your insurance)

Net Credit = Call premium received - Put premium paid
If net credit is positive, you earn money just from entering the position.

Payoff Diagram

Profit │ ╱ Stock only (no collar) │ ╱ Call caps upside →│─ ─ ─●━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ ← Max profit (call strike - entry + net credit) │ ╱ ┃ │ ╱ ┃ ──────────────────────●─────┃────── Stock Price ╱ │ ┃ ╱ │ Put limits loss →━━━━━●─ ─ ─ ← Max loss (entry - put strike - net credit) │ Loss ● Entry price ━━━ Collar payoff ╱ Stock-only payoff

Monthly Cycle

Our strategy runs in monthly cycles:

  1. Month start: Check if premiums are attractive enough (net credit ≥ threshold)
  2. If yes: Enter the collar — buy stock, sell call, buy put
  3. If no: Skip the month entirely — no position, no risk
  4. Month end: Square off everything — sell stock, buy back call, sell put
  5. Repeat next month

Example Trade

NIFTY at 22,000 | IV = 14% | 22 trading days to month end

Sell 22,200 Call → receive ₹259 premium (1.18% of entry)
Buy 20,900 Put → pay ₹19 premium (0.09% of entry)
Net Credit = ₹240 (1.09% of entry)

Scenario A: NIFTY ends at 22,100 (flat/up slightly)
Stock P&L: +100 | Call expires worthless: +259 | Put expires worthless: -19
Total P&L: +₹340 (+1.55%)

Scenario B: NIFTY rises to 23,000 (big rally, upside capped)
Stock P&L: +1,000 | Call loss: 259 - 800 = -541 | Put worthless: -19
Total P&L: +₹440 (+2.00%) (capped at call strike)

Scenario C: NIFTY falls to 21,000 (crash, put protects)
Stock P&L: -1,000 | Call worthless: +259 | Put gain: 0 - 19 = -19 (OTM put, no protection yet)
Total P&L: -₹760 (-3.45%)
Without the collar, loss would be -₹1,000 (-4.55%). If put were closer (e.g., 21,500), loss is further limited.

Why It Works

  • Theta decay: Each day, the call you sold loses value → profit for you
  • Volatility Risk Premium: Options are systematically overpriced vs realized moves
  • High win rate: Market stays flat or moves slightly most months → you keep the premium
  • Defined risk: The put limits your maximum loss

4. Volatility Risk Premium (VRP)

The Volatility Risk Premium is the most important concept in options selling. It's the persistent gap between implied volatility (what options are priced at) and realized volatility (what actually happens).

Why does VRP exist?

Options are insurance contracts. Just like car insurance costs more than the expected payout (the insurance company needs a margin), options cost more than the expected stock movement.

People pay extra for protection against crashes, earnings surprises, etc. As an option seller, you collect this extra. Most months, the stock moves less than what the options priced in → you keep the difference.
NIFTY Typical Range
Historical (realized) volatility8-14%
Implied volatility (India VIX)12-18%
VRP (the gap)~20-50% higher IV vs realized

In the backtester, we only have historical prices, not real option prices. So we use Black-Scholes to estimate premiums. You can set the IV two ways:

  • Fixed IV (recommended): Set the implied vol directly (e.g., 35%). This is what you see in the option chain. Simple and realistic.
  • VRP mode: Multiply historical vol by a factor. Less intuitive — historical vol varies month to month, and you need very high multipliers (200%+) to match real premiums.

Use Fixed IV = 30-40% for NIFTY to match real-world premiums of 3-5% on calls.

7. Practical Tips

Getting Started
  1. Select NIFTY 50 as the symbol
  2. Set IV source to Fixed IV and set it to 35% — this is critical for realistic premiums
  3. Set Min Premium to 0.75-1.0%
  4. Use Max Net Credit as the objective (matches manual trading)
  5. Run with 2 year period first, then try 5 years for robustness

Key Insights

  • VRP is your edge: The entire strategy profits from the gap between implied and realized vol. If VRP = 0 in the backtester, you're not modeling reality.
  • Months you skip matter: The premium gate protects you from entering low-premium months where the risk/reward isn't worth it. Don't set the threshold too low.
  • Drawdowns happen: Even with a 75%+ win rate, you will have months with 3-5% losses when the stock moves sharply. The put limits catastrophic loss, but doesn't eliminate all drawdowns.
  • Call strike matters: Closer to ATM = more premium but more likely to be breached. Further OTM = less premium but more upside room. The backtester scans the full range to find the best trade-off.
  • Put should be cheap: The put is insurance, not a profit center. Deep OTM puts (5%+ below) cost very little and protect against tail events.
  • B-S is an approximation: Real markets have bid-ask spreads, slippage, and liquidity effects. Treat backtest results as directional guides, not exact predictions.
